1 Integration in Finite Terms - Maxwell Rosenlicht

1.1 Aim

We aim to find if the indefinite integral of an explicitly given function of one variable always be expressed “explicitly” (or “in closed form”, or “in finite terms”). Liouville gave the answer one would expect, “No”, and he proved in particular that such is not the R 2 case with ex dx. ”explicitly given function” refers to a function that is expressed in terms of elementary operations and compositions of well-known, standard mathematical functions.

1.2 Prerequisites:

Elementary Functions:

Functions of one variable built up by using that variable and constants, together with repeated algebraic operations and the taking of exponentials and logarithms. Since we lose no generality by doing so, we shall take all exponentials and logarithms to the base e. We allow ourselves the convenience of the use of complex numbers, for with these the various trigonometric and inverse trigonometric functions turn out to be elementary.

Simplifying the problem

The functions we work with must be specific objects, each susceptible of an unambiguous sense. We choose to avoid the difficulties associated with multivaluedness by the simplest method, that of restricting ourselves, in any given discussion, to functions on some specific region (that is, nonempty connected open subset) of the real numbers R or the complex numbers C, and furthermore considering only meromorphic functions on the region in question. example of a multivalued function:- log(z) where z ∈ C takes multiple values, each differing by an integral multiple of 2πi

Meromorphic functions:

A meromorphic function on a region is a function whose values are complex numbers or the symbol ∞ , with the property that sufficiently near any point z, of the region the function is given by a convergent Laurent series in z - z0 that is, a convergent power series in z - z0 with the possible addition of a finite number of negative powers.

Differential Field:

A differential field is defined as a field F equipped with a derivation, which is a map F → F typically denoted by a 7→ a′ . This derivation must satisfy the following properties for all a, b ∈ F : (a + b)′ = a′ + b′ and (ab)′ = a′ b + ab′ .

From these axioms, several important consequences follow. First, for any a, b ∈ F with b ̸= 0, the quotient rule holds:

(a/b)′ = (a′ b − ab′ )/b2 .

Second, for any integer n, the power rule is satisfied:

(an )′ = nan−1 a′ .

Additionally, since 1′ = (12 )′ = 2·1·1′ , it follows that 1′ = 0. Consequently, the constants of F (i.e., all elements c ∈ F such that c′ = 0) form a subfield of F .

Logrithms and Exponentials:

Given elements a, b ∈ F with a ̸= 0, we say a is an exponential of b or b is a logarithm of a if b′ = a′ /a. This terminology is justified by the differential properties of exponentials and logarithms that are relevant here. Field Extension:

A field extension L/K is a pair of fields where K is a subfield of L (written K ⊆ L). It √ generalizes the idea of ”enlarging” a field by adjoining new elements (e.g., adding 2 to Q). Algeraic Extension: A field extension L/K is called algebraic if every element α ∈ L is algebraic over K. This means that for every α ∈ L, there exists a non-zero polynomial p(x) ∈ K[x] such that: p(α) = 0.

If L/K is not algebraic, it is called a transcendental extension. Finite Extension:L is said to be Finite over K if it has finite dimensions as a vector space over K.

1.7 Key Implications

The implicit function theorem guarantees that for a polynomial equation with meromor- phic coefficients (where the leading coefficient is non-zero), there exists a meromorphic solution on an appropriate subregion. Any elementary function composed of:

  • Algebraic operations

  • Exponentials

  • Logarithms

can be realized as a meromorphic function on some suitable region. The totality of all meromorphic functions on a given region forms a field under the usual operations of functional addition and multiplication, and the restriction of all these functions to any given subregion gives an embedding of fields. The derivative of a function meromorphic on a given region is again meromorphic, as is an indefinite integral, if one exists, of the function. Note that the rational functions on a region, that is, the restriction of C(z) to this region, are a field of meromorphic functions on the region that are closed under differenti- ation. Moreover, if we have any field of meromorphic functions on a region that is closed under differentiation and obtain a larger field of meromorphic functions on the region by adjoining:

  • The exponential or a logarithm of a function in our field, or

  • A solution of a polynomial equation with coefficients in the field,

we again get a field of meromorphic functions on the region that is closed under differen- tiation. Thus, the proper objects of study are fields of meromorphic functions on given regions in R or C which are closed under differentiation. If a function in such a field has an indefinite integral that is expressible “in finite terms,” then by restricting all functions (if necessary) to a suitable subregion, we obtain a tower of such fields of meromorphic functions. Each larger field in the tower is obtained by adjunction of:

  • An exponential, or

  • A logarithm, or

  • The solution of an algebraic equation,

with the tower starting with the original field and culminating in a field containing the indefinite integral. Thus, the original loosely worded analytic problem, when formulated as a precise analytic problem, becomes algebraic.

2 A simple Algorithm for Cyclic vectors - Nicholas M. Katz

2.1 Aim

In this paper, we present a simple and effective algorithm for constructing cyclic vectors in the context of differential modules over commutative rings. Given a free module V of rank n equipped with a derivation D, a cyclic vector is an element v ∈ V such that the iterated derivatives {v, Dv, . . . , Dn−1 v} form a basis for V . Our algorithm exploits the invertibility of (n − 1)! in the base ring R and relies on a distinguished element t ∈ R satisfying ∂(t) = 1 for a derivation ∂. The key construction involves the explicit formula:

n−1 j (t − a)j X   X k j c(⃗e, t − a) = (−1) Dk (ej−k ), j=0 j! k=0 k

where ⃗e = (e0 , . . . , en−1 ) is a basis of V , and a is a “constant” (i.e., ∂(a) = 0). Under mild hypotheses—including the condition that R is a local Z[1/(n − 1)!]-algebra and t − a lies in the maximal ideal—we prove that c(⃗e, t − a) is cyclic. A notable application arises when R contains a sufficiently large field k: if #k > n(n − 1), then Zariski-locally on Spec(R), at least one of the vectors c(⃗e, t − ai ) (for distinct ai ∈ k) is cyclic. This result provides a computationally tractable method to detect cyclic vectors, with implications for the structure theory of differential equations and connections in algebraic geometry.

2.2 Prerequisites

  1. R-algebra:Let R be a commutative ring with identity. An R-algebra is a ring A with identity together with a ring homomorphism f : R → A mapping 1R → 1A such that the subring f (R) of A is contained in the center of A.

  2. Z[1/(n − 1)!]-algebra:A commutative ring R equipped with a ring homomorphism Z[1/(n − 1)!] → R. This ensures that (n − 1)! is invertible in R, making all smaller factorials j! (for j ≤ n − 1) invertible as well.

  3. Local Ring:A commutative ring with a unique maximal ideal m . The condition t − a ∈ m means t ≡ a (mod m).

  4. Cyclic Vector:An element v ∈ V is cyclic for (V, D) if v, Dv, . . . , Dn−1 v forms an R-basis of V . This generalizes the notion of a cyclic vector in linear algebra. A basis of this form is called a cyclic basis.

  5. Nakayama’s Lemma:Let R be a commutative ring with identity, I ⊂ R an ideal contained in the Jacobson radical of R, and M a finitely generated R-module. If IM = M , then M = 0.

Key Corollary

If N ⊂ M is a submodule and IM + N = M , then M = N .

Application in the Proof

In the given context, Nakayama’s Lemma is used to show that:

  • The vectors {Di c(⃗e, t − a)}i=0 n−1 generate V modulo (t − a)V
  • Since t − a belongs to the maximal ideal (by hypothesis), and V is finitely generated, the lemma implies these vectors actually generate V
Why It Works
  • The condition Di c(⃗e, t − a) ≡ ei mod (t − a)V shows the vectors are linearly independent
  • Nakayama’s lemma lifts this to a full basis over R

  1. Wedge product:For vectors v1 , . . . , vk in an R-module V , their wedge product is the antisymmetric tensor

    v1 ∧ · · · ∧ vk ∈ Λk (V )

where Λk (V ) is the k-th exterior power of V .

Key Properties

(a) Antisymmetry:

v ∧ w = −w ∧ v       (implies v ∧ v = 0)

(b) Multilinearity:

(av + bw) ∧ u = a(v ∧ u) + b(w ∧ u)

(c) Basis Representation: If {e1 , . . . , en } is a basis for V , then

{ei1 ∧ · · · ∧ eik | 1 ≤ i1 < · · · < ik ≤ n}

forms a basis for Λk (V ).

Role in the Proof

In the given context:

  • The wedge product detects linear independence:

    c0 ∧ · · · ∧ cn−1 ̸= 0 ⇐⇒ {ci } are linearly independent

  • The computation:

    c0 (⃗e, X) ∧ · · · ∧ cn−1 (⃗e, X) = P (X)e0 ∧ · · · ∧ en−1

shows that P (X) is the determinant of the transformation from {ei } to {ci }.

  • P (t − a) ∈ R× ensures the determinant is invertible, making {Di c(⃗e, t − a)} a basis.
Geometric Interpretation

The non-vanishing of v1 ∧ · · · ∧ vn corresponds to:

  • Non-zero volume of the parallelotope formed by the vectors
  • Linear independence in differential geometry
  • Orientation of a basis in topology
